\subsubsection{Approximating exponentials}

\paragraph{Results}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{approx_exp1_0.pdf}
    \caption{Truncation errors when approximating $e^{x}$ through the
				truncated power series $\Sigma^{N}_{0}$. $N$ is the degree of the polinomyal used
				for the approximation. The plot of $x^{N+1}/(N+1)$ has been
				added for reference.}
    \label{fig:approx_exp}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Remarks}

\begin{itemize}
	\item{Horner's method was used in polynomial evaluation, because it is
			faster and reduces roundoff errors. A naive implementation of a 
			polynomial computation requires more operations ($O(N^{2})$) and, 
			for small values of $x$, adds together numbers of order 1 and $x^{N}$,
			while Horner's method is $O(N)$ and keeps the additions only
			between terms of order 1 and x.}
	\item{By Taylor's theorem, if $\hat{f}(x)$ is the power series
			of $e^{x}$ truncated after the N-th term, the error is of order 
			$O(x^{N+1})$ as x approaches 0. Computing an additional term of the
			series shows that:
			\[|e^{x}-\hat{f}(x)|=\frac{x^{N+1}}{(N+1)!}+o(x^{N+1})\]
			It is then not surprising that the 
			approximation error in Figure \ref{fig:approx_exp} is 
			indistinguishable from $x^{N+1}/(N+1)$ for small values of x. For
			larger values, the contribution of higher order terms in the series 
			becomes ever more important, and hence the divergence from
			$x^{N+1}/(N+1)$.}
			
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Computing the Basel problem}

\paragraph{Results}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{basel1_2_1float.pdf}
    \caption{Approximation error when computing $\pi^{2}/6$ with the Basel problem truncated
		series $\Sigma_0^{N}\frac{1}{n^{2}}$, with single precision floating variables. Here $\mathrm{N}$ is 
		the number of terms used when calculating the series.}
    \label{fig:baselfloat}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{basel1_2_1double.pdf}
    \caption{Same as above, but now using double precision floating variables.
		the plot of $1/x$ has been added for reference.}
    \label{fig:baseldouble}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Remarks}

\begin{itemize}
	\item{
		The algorithm has been implemented with two mathematically equivalent 
		forms, which, however, resulted in remarkably different numerical
		results. \\
		In the first the terms of the series are summed in increasing order of 
		the summation index, i.e. decreasing of the value of the addends. Since
		small addends are summed to the accumulating partial sum, it gets to a 
		point where the next addend is smaller than $\epsilon_{mach} \cdot sum \approx \epsilon_{mach}$,
		so the addition of it and subsequent values does not change the value of the partial sum. \\
		This can be seen in the error plots in Figure \ref{fig:baselfloat} and \ref{fig:baseldouble},
		since the error stops decreasing and remains constant after $ \mathrm{1/N^{2}} = \epsilon_{ \mathrm{mach} } $,
		which is $2^{-24}$ for single precision (N = 4096) and $2^{-53}$ for double precision ($\mathrm{N} \approx 9.5 \times 10^{7}).$ \\
		
		In the second implementation the terms of the series are summed in decreasing
		order of the summation index, i.e. increasing the value of the addends. Therefore,
		the aforementioned effect is not seen, and the numerical results correspond 
		to the mathematical expectation, in the range considered.
	}
	\item{
		The truncation error of this algorithm is asymptotically equivalent to
		$k/N$, with k real constant. This can be from the integral associated
		to $ \Sigma_{n=0}^{\infty} $:
		\[ \int^{\infty}_{N} \frac{1}{n^{2}} = \frac{1}{N}\]
		therefore,
		
		\[ \Sigma_{n=N}^{\infty} \sim_{N \to \infty } \frac{1}{N} \]
		
		which is confirmed from the agreement between the computed values and
		the $1/x$ reference line in Figure \ref{fig:baseldouble}.
		}
\end{itemize}

\begin{itemize}
	\item{
		The exact LU-factorization of $\mathbf{A}$ is:
		\[
			\mathbf{A}=
			\begin{bmatrix}
				1 & 0 \\
				-\epsilon^{-1} & 1
			\end{bmatrix}
			\begin{bmatrix}
				-\epsilon & 1 \\
				0 & \epsilon^{-1} - 1
			\end{bmatrix}
		\]
		If $\epsilon=10^{-20}\ll \epsilon_{\text{mach}}$, in the bottom-right entry
		of $\mathbf{U}$ only $\epsilon^{-1}$ gets stored, since the relative 
		variation of adding -1 to $\epsilon^{-1}$ is less than
		 $\epsilon_{\text{mach}}$.
		But when $\mathbf{L}$ and $\mathbf{U}$ get
		multiplied together, in the addition resulting into the bottom-right
		entry of $\mathbf{A}$ two $\epsilon^{-1}$ terms cancels out, and
		the exact result is the -1 that is lost in numerical LU-decomposition.
	}
	\item{
		Exact $\mathbf{b}$ is:
		\[
			\begin{bmatrix} 1 -\epsilon \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} 
		\]
		but $\epsilon$ does not get registered if it is lower than 
		$\epsilon_{\text{mach}}$.
		Because the condition number of the matrices $\mathbf{L}$ and $\mathbf{U}$
		is very high ($\epsilon^{-2}$ in \infty-norm), the tiny variation of 
		$\mathbf{b_{\text{calc}}}$ over 
		$\mathbf{b_{\text{exact}}}$ is allowed to propagate tremendously and 
		results in the wrong result seen before.
		
		Doing the calculations shows that the error on $\mathbf{U}$ as no
		impact on $\mathbf{b_{\text{calc}}}$, and that the final error on 
		$\mathbf{b_{\text{calc}}}$ is due entirely on the ill-conditioning

\subsubsection{Fitting of Kepler's Law}

We want to extrapolate Kepler's third law from experimental data through the
algorithm just introduced. First we linearize the following relationship by 
taking the logarithm at both sides:

\[
	\mathrm{T = C D^{\alpha} \implies \log(T) = \log(C) + \alpha \log(D) }
\]

where T is the orbital periods of the planets, D is their average distance from
the Sun and alpha and C are two costants to be determined through the method
of least squares. This is equivalent to finding $x_{1}$ and 
$x_{2}$ that minimize:
\[
	\mathbf{\log(T)} = x_{1}\mathbf{1} + x_{2}\mathbf{\log(D)} =
	\begin{bmatrix}
		\mathbf{1} & \mathbf{\log(D)}
	\end{bmatrix}
	\begin{bmatrix}
		x_{1} \\
		x_{2}
	\end{bmatrix}
\]

where quantities in bold are vectors, leading to an overdetermined linear system.

\paragraph{Results}

We therefore find that the values that fit the aforementioned system are:
\[
\alpha = 1.49865 \qquad C = 0.201522
\]

where alpha is consistent with Kepler's law, which predicts $\alpha = 1.5$.

\paragraph{Remarks}

		Not surpringly, the function is better approximated by the first terms

		approximating periodic functions, as they do blow up at infinity
		and do not approximate well outside of the points employed in the fit.
	}

\subsection{Polynomial interpolation by solving the Vandermonde matrix linear system}

Interpolating polynomials in the power basis by constructing the 
Vandermonde matrix and solving the related linear system is numerically
unstable. Tha matrix thus constructed is likely to be ill-conditioned,
due to the fact that its entries are the nth powers of x, and can become
quickly extremely large or small as the system scales in size.

In our case study, we considered equidistant point in the interval [-1,1]
generated by the function $\cos(kx)$.

In Figure AGGIUNGI REFERENCE we can see that the condition number scales
exponentially with the number of points considered in the fit, which is also the 
size of the linear system.

AGGIUNGI FIGURA

In our study we always considered $4n+1$ equidistant points, including the 
extrema of the interval -1 and 1. As a consequence, the middle point considered 
is always (0,1), since cos(0) = 1. Therefore, we must have:

\[
	P_{n}(0) = a_{0}+ a_{1}\cdot 0 + ... + a_{n}\cdot 0 = a_{0} = 1
\]



CERCA DI CAPIRE COME MOSTRARE I VALORI

If we were operating in exact arithmetic, this would produce a value of 
$a_{0}$ always equal to 1, since it would lead to a linear system where a row
of the Vandermonde matrix (the one corresponding to point x=0) which is 
equivalent to equation $a_{0}=1$. However, the LUP decomposition algorithm 
contaminates several times the aforementioned row of the linear system, relying
later on cancellation of possibly large values to recover the information of
$a_{0}=1$. Therefore, in finite-precision arithmetic the value of $a_{0}$ can 
differ from 1, as we can see ... . This is closely related to the 
ill-conditioning of the Vandermonde matrix.

\subsubsection{Chebyshev nodes interpolation of non-smooth functions}

Polynomial interpolants on Chebyshev nodes are guaranteed to converge 
exponentially to the reference function if the latter is analytic, as 
the number of nodes approaches infinity.
If it is not, convergence might not be guaranteed or it might be slower.

We can observe this slowdown by studying the convergence for the
family of functions $|x|^{m}$. These functions are not smooth on the interval
[-1, 1]. They can be differentiated $m-1$ times, but not $m$ times, since 
$|m|$ is not differentiable in $x=0$. Hence they are not analytic.
The results are shown in ADD REFERENCE AND FIGURES

\paragraph{Remarks}

\begin{itemize}
	\item{
		As previously said, $|x|^{m}$ is not analytical. However, their 
		derivatives are continuously differentiable up to $f^{(m-2)}$, 
		thus absolutely continuous. $f^{(m-1)} = C|x|$, which is Lipschitz
		continuous and as such it is absolutely continuous. On the other hand:
		\[
			f^{(m)}(x) = \begin{cases}
			  +C, & x > 0 \\
			  -C,  & x < 0
			  \end{cases}
		\]
		for some C>0. This function is monotonic, hence it is of bounded variation. This
		proves a theorem that states that if the above hypotheses are verified (absolute
		continuity up to $m-1$-th derivative and bounded variation of $m$-th 
		derivative), then the Chebyshev polynomial interpolant converges to the function
		in sup-norm as $\mathcal{O}(n^{-m})$, as the number of nodes n approaches 
		infinity.
	}
	\item{
		To compare this prediction with our results, we interpolated the 
		sup-norm error using algorithm from ADD REFERENCES
		By choosing $Y=\log_{10}(||f-p||_{\infty}) = \log_{10}(c) - K\log_{10}(n)$ 
		and $X = \log_{10}(n)$ we
		interpolated the relation:
		\[
			Y = C - K\,X
		\]
		where -K is the exponent in the asymptotic behaviour $\mathcal{O}(n^{-m})$
		of $||f-p||_{\infty}$. The fitting lines can are visualised in ADD REFERENCE
		as dashed lines. Our results are reported in the following table 
		and are in accordance to the prediction above:
		\begin{table}[ht]
			\centering
			\begin{tabular}{c|c}
				m & K \\
				\hline
				1 &  1.04\\
				3 &  3.13\\
				5 &  5.15\\
				7 &  7.26\\
				9 & 9.41\\
				11 & 11.7
			\end{tabular}
		\end{table}
	}
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Bisection method}\label{ssection:bis_method}

In order to test our routine for rootfinding with the bisection method,
we searched for the roots of the function $x^3-7x^2+14x-6$ in the interval 
[0, 1] and found $x = 0.5858$. The plot (which we omitted from the report, 
but can be visualized during code execution) of the function and the 
found root confirms that it is the only root in the interval.  

Given an iterative rootfinding algorithm, 
if we define $\epsilon_{n} = x_{n}-r$, where $r$
is the root that we are going to find and $x_{n}$ is the root estimate at step
n, then if 

\begin{equation}
	\lim_{n \to +\infty} \left| \frac{\epsilon_{n+1}}{\epsilon_{n}^{q}} \right |=C
\end{equation}

for $q \geq 1$ and $0<C<\infty$, we say that the algorithm converges to the root
with order of convergence $q$ and asymptotic constant $C$.

It is very easy to see that for the bisection algorithm $q=1$ and $C=\frac{1}{2}$.
Under this assumption, we get the asymptotic relation

\[
	|\epsilon_{n+1}| \approx |\frac{1}{2}\epsilon_{n}|
\]

which is the asymptotic recurrence relation of a geometric sequence 
$\epsilon_{n}=K(\frac{1}{2})^{n}$.

Defining $a_{n}=-\log_{10}(|\epsilon_{n}|)$ we get:

\[
	a_{n} = K' + n \log_{10}(2)
\]

In ADD FIGURE AND REFERENCE we plotted the $a_{n}$ as a function of $n$. 
The curve is a line, confirming that the order of convergence is 1. 
Fitting the curve with a line through least squares yields a slope of
1.992, which implies an asymptotic error constant $C = 0.5018$, 
in accordance with bisection method's convergence theory.

As a termination condition of the loop in the algorithm we check if:

\[
	|x_{1}-x_{2}| <= \text{tol} + \epsilon_{\text{mach}}\max(|a|,|b|)
\]

Where $|a|$ and $|b|$ are the boundaries of the starting interval and 
$x_{1}$, $x_{2}$ are the boundaries of the interval at current step.

\mdcode{tol} can be specified by the caller or instead be 
$10\epsilon{\text{mach}}$ if the caller inserts the macro \mdcode{DEF_TOL}
as parameter.

$\epsilon_{\text{mach}}\max(|a|,|b|)$ is added because it is approximately the
distance between the largest of the two starting boundaries (in absolute value).
The algorithm is not always able to bisect the algorithm further when it is of 
the order of the quantity above, so it is a safety check.

\paragraph{Remarks}

\begin{itemize}
	\item{
		If a root $r$ of a function $f(x)$ satisfies $f'(r) \neq 0$ and 
		$f''(r) \neq 0$, then Newton's method's convergence order is quadratic
		for that root and the asymptotic error constant is:
\[
	C = \frac{1}{2}\left|\frac{f''(r)}{f'(r)}\right|
\]
		That is the case for the root of $f_{1}$ and for root $x = 1.166$ of
		$f_{2}$. In both cases the estimated convergence order is close to 2
		and the estimated asymptotic error constant agrees with the one
		calculated analitically by 4+ significant digits. Hence, we do not
		include separately the analitical values in the report and we refer
		the reader to the program \mdcode{newton_method_error_study.c}.
	}
	\item{
		If the root is not simple, i.e. $f'(r) = 0$, but $f''(x) \neq 0$, then
		it can be shown that convergence is linear with asymptotic error 
		constant $C = 0.5$. Root the root of $f_{3}$ satisfies this condition.
		In our analysis we see that most ratios $a_{n+1}/a_{n}$ agree wih 0.5,
		except for the last few values.
	}
	\item{
		For root $r=0$ of $f_{3}$ the estimated order of convergence is about 3.
		Seeing that for this function $f''(r)=0$, this prompted us to check
		with a small computation whether $f''(r)=0$ implies order of 
		convergence 3. We start from the relation:
		\[
			\epsilon_{k+1} = \epsilon_{k} - \frac{f(r + \epsilon_{k})}{f'(r + \epsilon_{k})}
		\]
		which is a rewrite of Newton's algorithm.
		Expanding with Taylor's:
		\[
			\epsilon_{k+1} = \epsilon_{k} - 	
			\frac{f(r) + f'(r)\epsilon_{k} + 
			\frac{1}{2}\epsilon_{k}^{2}f''(r) + \frac{1}{6}\epsilon_{k}^{3}f'''(r) +
			O(\epsilon_{k}^{4})}{f'(r) + f''(r)\epsilon_{k} + 
			\frac{1}{2}\epsilon_{k}^{2}f'''(r) + O(\epsilon_{k}^{3})} = 
		\]
		\[
			= \epsilon_{k} - 	
			\frac{f'(r)\epsilon_{k} +  \frac{1}{6}\epsilon_{k}^{3}f'''(r) +
			O(\epsilon_{k}^{4})}
			{f'(r) + \frac{1}{2}\epsilon_{k}^{2}f'''(r) + O(\epsilon_{k}^{3})} =
		\]
		Collecting $f'(r)$:
		\[
			= \epsilon_{k} \left[ 1 - \left( 1 + \frac{1}{6} \epsilon_{k}^{2}
			\frac{f'''(r)}{f'(r)}\right)\left( 1 + \frac{1}{2}\epsilon_{k}^{2}
			\frac{f'''(r)}{f'(r)}\right)^{-1}\right] +O(\epsilon_{k}^{4})=
		\]
		By doing some simple algebra:
		\[
			=\frac{1}{3}\epsilon_{k}^{3}\frac{f'''(r)}{f'(r)}+O(\epsilon_{k}^{4})
		\]
		i.e. cubic convergence, with asymptotic error constant 
		$C = \frac{1}{3}f'''(r)/f'(r)$.
		By executing \mdcode{newton_method_error_study}, the reader can see that 
		the numerically estimated and the analitically calculated one agree by
		4 significant digits, after only 6 steps have been taken by Newton's 
		method.
	}
\end{itemize}



\subsubsection{Which root gets found by Newton's method?}

Newton method, in general, is not globally convergent. Moreover, if two or more 
roots are present, if convergent there is no guarantee that the root that gets
found is the one "closest" to the starting value for the algorithm.

In this paragraph we study the relationship between the starting value and the
root that gets found by the method. We use as case study the function 
$f(x) = x^{-2} + \sin(x)$ and as starting values for the algorithm 
$x = 1, 2, ..., 7$.

In ADD FIGURE AND REFERENCES we have plotted $f(x)$, along with the starting
values and the related roots that get found.

We see that the initial guesses 2 and 5 produce return roots that are not
the closest to the starting points. 
In order to understand what is going on, we have drawn the tangent line 
to the graph at each of those two initial guesses. Newton method approximates
the function to that line at the first iteration.
We can see from the graph the tangent lines drawn intersect the x axis 
"far away" from the starting guess, since at both points the function has
a slope small in absolute value.

If we include bracketing in our algorithm, then we can be sure that the 
root that gets found will be inside of the interval from which we started.

ADD FIGURE AND REFERENCE shows the root found inside the arbitrality chosen 
intervals $[0.5,\,2]$, $[2,\,5]$, $[5,\,8]$ and $[8,\,10]$.

\subsubsection{Eros' true anomaly}

As an application of Newton method, we seek to evaluate the \textit{true anomaly}
$\theta(t)$ of asteroid Eros, defined as:

\[
	\tan{\frac{\theta}{2}} = \sqrt{\frac{1+\epsilon}{1-\epsilon}}
	\tan{\frac{\psi}{2}}
\]

For that, we need to evaluate first the \textit{eccentric anomaly} $\psi(t)$ 
which can be found by solving the equaton:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:eccentric_anomaly}
	f(\psi) = \psi - \epsilon \sin{\psi} - \frac{2\pi t}{\tau} = 0
\end{equation}

For asteroid Eros, we have \textit{orbial period} $\tau = 1.7610$ and
\textit{elliptical eccentricity} $\epsilon = 0.2230$.

Derivative of equation \ref{eq:eccentric_anomaly} with respect to $\psi$ is:

\[
	f'(\psi) = 1 - \epsilon \cos{\psi}
\]

Since $\epsilon < 1$, $f'(\psi)>0$ for any $\psi$, the function is monotonic
and we need not worry about finding multiple roots. Also, by plotting function 
\ref{eq:eccentric_anomaly} we see that it is reasonably well-behaved, and it
might be even reasonable to expect global convergence.
Nonetheless, we can also simply observe that for $\psi$ such that $f(\psi) = 0$:

\[
	\left|\psi - \frac{2\pi t}{\tau}\right| = 
	\left| \epsilon \sin{\psi} \right| \leq \epsilon
\]

that is, if we safeguard Newton method with bracketing and we take as starting
interval $[\frac{2\pi t}{\tau} - \epsilon, \frac{2\pi t}{\tau} + \epsilon]$
then we are guaranteed to find the root.
